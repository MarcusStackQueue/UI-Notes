## Part A — Multiple Choice (50 pts)

### Pre-Midterm MCQs (Q1–Q20)

**Q1.** A designer writes "Aisha, 34, bus driver, has 20 min between shifts to check her union's schedule app on her phone." This is best described as a:

- A) Negative persona
- B) Primary persona anatomy entry
- C) Scenario setting
- D) Use case precondition

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C) Scenario setting.** The description is a short narrative about *context* (where, when, constraints) — that's the Setting element of a scenario, not a persona (no goals/tasks listed) and not a use case (no actor/system interaction).

</details>

---

**Q2.** Which of the following is NOT one of Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics?

- A) Visibility of system status
- B) Aesthetic and minimalist design
- C) Accessibility for assistive technologies
- D) Match between system and the real world

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C) Accessibility for assistive technologies.** Accessibility is handled by WCAG, not Nielsen. The other three are all in the canonical 10.

</details>

---

**Q3.** A user clicks "Delete Account," and the system immediately deletes it with no confirmation dialog. Which heuristic is most directly violated?

- A) Recognition rather than recall
- B) User control and freedom
- C) Flexibility and efficiency of use
- D) Help and documentation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B) User control and freedom.** Users need "emergency exits" and the ability to undo destructive actions. A confirmation dialog (or undo) is the standard way to support this.

</details>

**Q4.** The purpose of a **negative persona** is to:

- A) Identify users who hate your product

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B) Clarify who you are not designing for, to prevent scope creep.** Negative personas are a scope tool, not a disrespect tool.

**Q25.** Fitts' Law predicts that the time required to acquire a target is a function of:

- A) The target's color and the user's age
- B) The distance to the target and the target's size
- C) The number of other targets on screen
- D) The user's familiarity with the interface

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B) The distance to the target and the target's size.** Formally T = a + b·log₂(D/W + 1). Targets that are bigger and closer are faster to hit.

</details>

---

**Q26.** A designer places the "Close Window" button in the top-right *corner* of the screen (macOS top-left). Which UX law justifies this?

- A) Hick's Law
- B) Miller's Law
- C) Fitts' Law
- D) Jakob's Law

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C) Fitts' Law.** Screen corners have effectively infinite size because the cursor can't overshoot — the edge stops it. This makes corners the fastest targets to hit.

</details>

---

**Q27.** A settings menu with 40 flat items on one page is redesigned into 7 grouped categories of 4–6 items each. Which law most directly justifies the redesign?

- A) Fitts' Law
- B) Hick's Law
- C) Jakob's Law
- D) Postel's Law

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B) Hick's Law.** Decision time grows logarithmically with the number of choices. Grouping reduces the visible choice count at each level.

</details>

**Q38.** Likert scale data (e.g., 1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree) is technically which type of data?

- D) Ratio

**Q39.** You run a usability study comparing task time on Design A vs. Design B with 20 participants in each condition. Which statistical test is most appropriate?

## Part B — True / False (20 pts)

### Pre-Midterm T/F (Q51–Q58)

**Q51.** A scenario and a use case are interchangeable terms for the same artifact.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** A scenario is a *narrative* answering "what happens in real life?" A use case is a *structured actor/system spec* answering "what must the system do?" — with preconditions, main flow, alternate flows, and postconditions.

</details>

---

**Q52.** A primary persona represents the user you most want to satisfy with your design.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.** Primary personas are the main target — if you satisfy them, you're winning.

</details>

---

**Q53.** An open card sort provides participants with pre-made categories into which they place cards.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** That's a *closed* card sort. Open card sorts let participants invent and label their own categories.

</details>

---

**Q54.** The Gestalt principle of Similarity says that elements sharing visual properties (color, shape, size) are perceived as belonging together.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.**

</details>

---

**Q55.** Nielsen's heuristics are empirical laws with precise numerical thresholds, not rules of thumb.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** They are heuristics — rules of thumb. They guide judgment; they don't replace user testing.

</details>

---

**Q56.** Information Architecture is only concerned with visual design choices like color and typography.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** IA is about *structure* — how content is organized, labeled, and made findable. Visual design is a separate layer.

</details>

---

**Q57.** A use case's "alternative flow" describes what happens when the user deviates from the main happy path or an exception occurs.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.** Main flow = success path; alternative/exception flows cover deviations and errors.

</details>

---

**Q58.** "Recognition rather than recall" means users should be forced to memorize menu paths to prove mastery.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** The heuristic says the opposite — minimize memory load by making options visible and recognizable instead of making users recall them from memory.

</details>

---

### Post-Midterm T/F (Q59–Q70)

**Q59.** "Confirmshaming" is a dark pattern that uses emotionally loaded wording on the decline option to shame the user.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.** "No thanks, I don't care about saving money" is the textbook example.

</details>

---

**Q60.** According to Fitts' Law, the time to hit a target increases linearly with distance and decreases linearly with size.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** The relationship is *logarithmic*, not linear: T = a + b·log₂(D/W + 1).

</details>

---

**Q61.** Jakob's Law implies designers should reject web conventions in favor of novel UI patterns on every project.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** Jakob's Law implies the opposite — users expect familiar conventions because they've been trained by every other site they use. Novelty for its own sake increases cognitive load.

</details>

---

**Q62.** WCAG 2.1 Level AAA is the minimum legal standard commonly required for public-sector websites.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** Level AA is the typical legal/organizational standard. AAA is aspirational and not achievable for all content types.

</details>

---

**Q63.** Providing captions for pre-recorded video satisfies a Perceivable requirement of WCAG.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.** Captions make audio content perceivable to users who are deaf or hard of hearing.

</details>

---

**Q64.** Contextual inquiry and in-lab usability testing produce equivalent data, so the methods are interchangeable.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** Contextual inquiry happens in the user's real environment and captures workflow/workarounds/interruptions; in-lab usability testing evaluates a specific interface under controlled conditions. Different questions, different methods.

</details>

---

**Q65.** A p-value below 0.05 always means the effect is practically important.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** A small p-value indicates *statistical* significance, which only means the effect is unlikely to be due to chance. Effect size (e.g., Cohen's d) measures *practical* importance. A huge sample can make a trivial effect statistically significant.

</details>

---

**Q66.** An independent-samples t-test is appropriate for comparing task-completion times between two separate groups of users.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**True.** Two independent groups + continuous outcome = t-test.

</details>

---

**Q67.** Thematic analysis is a quantitative technique that produces p-values.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** Thematic analysis is qualitative — it identifies patterns/themes in text data. No p-values involved.

</details>

---

**Q68.** No-code platforms like Webflow and Bubble completely eliminate the need for any design-systems thinking.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** No-code tools speed up *production* but don't replace IA, content strategy, visual hierarchy, or accessibility thinking. Garbage in, garbage out — even faster now.

</details>

---

**Q69.** In the Smallville paper, emergent social behaviors (a Valentine's Day party, coordinated dates) were hard-coded into each agent's script.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** The emergent behaviors were *not* scripted. From a single seed (one agent wanting to throw a Valentine's Day party) the invitations, coordination, and date-making emerged from the agents' architecture interacting over simulated time.

</details>

---

**Q70.** The Smallville agents use only recency to decide which memories to retrieve from the memory stream.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Q71.** **Dark Patterns (5 pts).** Name three distinct dark patterns, briefly explain how each one manipulates the user, and for *one* of the three suggest a specific redesign that preserves the business goal without the manipulation.

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

A strong answer names three clearly distinct patterns from the lecture's list of 10:

- **Roach Motel** — the service is easy to sign up for but extremely difficult to cancel (multi-step cancellation flows, phone-only cancellation, etc.). Manipulates users by exploiting the asymmetry between signup effort and cancellation effort.
- **Misdirection / False Hierarchy** — visual design intentionally steers attention toward the option that benefits the company (e.g., a large green "Accept All Cookies" button next to a tiny grey "Reject" link). Exploits perceptual bias.
- **Confirmshaming** — the decline option uses emotionally loaded or self-deprecating language ("No thanks, I don't want to save money") to guilt the user into agreeing.

**Redesign example (Roach Motel):** A business still needs to retain subscribers, but can achieve that ethically by making cancellation a single-screen flow that mirrors signup, then offering a *non-coercive* pause or discount option *after* the user confirms intent to cancel. This respects user autonomy while still giving the business a fair retention opportunity.

*Grading note:* You need three distinct mechanisms (not three flavors of misdirection) and your redesign should genuinely preserve the business goal — not just "remove the pattern."

</details>

**Q72.** **UX Laws (5 pts).** Explain Fitts' Law and Hick's Law. For each, describe one specific UI design decision the law predicts, and briefly note how the two laws can *conflict* in a real interface.

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

**Fitts' Law** states that the time to acquire a target is a logarithmic function of the distance to and size of the target: T = a + b·log₂(D/W + 1). Targets that are larger and closer to the user's current pointer position are faster to acquire. *Design implication:* primary actions should be large and placed near the user's focus or at screen edges/corners (which have effectively infinite width because the cursor cannot overshoot).

**Hick's Law** states that decision time increases logarithmically with the number of choices presented: T = b·log₂(n + 1). *Design implication:* reduce the number of visible options, group related items, and use progressive disclosure so the user decides among a smaller set at each step.

**Conflict:** Fitts' Law pushes toward *fewer, larger* targets, while Hick's Law pushes toward *fewer* choices — but in practice designers often try to surface many common actions quickly, which adds more targets (hurting Hick) or shrinks each target (hurting Fitts). A toolbar packed with 24 small icons is a classic case: each icon is hard to hit (Fitts penalty) *and* the user has to scan 24 options to pick one (Hick penalty). The resolution is usually hierarchical organization — a small set of primary, large targets at the top level, with progressive disclosure of secondary actions.

</details>

**Q73.** **WCAG — POUR (5 pts).** Name the four WCAG principles. For each, give a concrete example of a design failure that violates it.

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

The four WCAG principles are **Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust**.

- **Perceivable** — information must be available to users through more than one sense. *Failure:* product images have no alt text, so screen reader users cannot perceive them. Another: body text at 2:1 contrast on a pale background fails the 4.5:1 AA contrast ratio.
- **Operable** — UI components must be operable by a range of inputs. *Failure:* a custom dropdown that opens only on mouse hover and cannot be reached or activated via keyboard Tab/Enter, locking out users who don't use a mouse.
- **Understandable** — content and operation must be understandable. *Failure:* form error messages that say only "Error 47" with no explanation of what went wrong or how to fix it.
- **Robust** — content must work across user agents, including assistive technologies. *Failure:* a button implemented as a styled `<div>` with no ARIA role, so screen readers announce it as plain text rather than a button, breaking interaction for AT users.

</details>

**Q74.** **Research Methods (5 pts).** You've been asked to evaluate a new signup flow before launch. Describe a mixed-methods study design that uses at least one qualitative and one quantitative method, and explain what each method contributes that the other cannot.

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

I'd combine a **think-aloud usability test** (qualitative) with a **quantitative metrics pass** (completion rate, task time, drop-off by step) on an A/B test of the new flow against the current one.

**Think-aloud (qualitative)** with 6–8 participants surfaces *why* users hesitate, get confused, or abandon — the specific wording on a label that looks like a required field but isn't, the password rule that appears only after submission, the moment a user re-reads the privacy notice and pauses. This reveals causes that numbers alone never show.

**Quantitative metrics (A/B test)** with a larger sample (ideally several hundred users per arm) tells us *whether* the new flow actually improves signup completion rate at scale, and where users drop off. I'd report both the p-value and effect size (and a confidence interval on the difference in conversion rates) so we're not fooled by a statistically significant but trivially small improvement.

The qualitative study cannot generalize to the full user base; the quantitative study cannot explain *why* users behave the way they do. Together they answer both "is the new flow better?" and "what should we fix next?" That's the core value of mixed methods — quant finds the *what*, qual explains the *why*.

</details>

**Q75.** **Smallville Paper — Architecture (5 pts).** The Smallville paper introduces a three-component architecture that extends an LLM to produce believable agent behavior. Name the three components and briefly explain what each one contributes. Why is a simple "feed the whole memory stream back into the prompt" approach insufficient?

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

The three components sit on top of a **memory stream** — a running natural-language log of the agent's experiences.

1. **Memory retrieval.** Rather than feeding the entire memory stream back to the LLM (which would be too large and dominated by irrelevant history), retrieval ranks memories by a combination of *recency* (more recent = more accessible), *importance* (each memory carries an LLM-assigned importance score at storage time), and *relevance* (semantic similarity to the current situation). Only the top-ranked memories enter the prompt.
2. **Reflection.** Periodically — when the summed importance of recent memories crosses a threshold — the agent is prompted to ask "what are the most salient high-level questions we can answer about these observations?" and generates abstract insights ("Klaus Mueller is deeply invested in his research on gentrification"). Reflections themselves are stored in the memory stream and can build on earlier reflections, producing a tree of self-knowledge.
3. **Planning.** The agent generates a top-down plan that starts as a broad 5–8 point daily outline and recursively decomposes into hourly and 5–15 minute intervals, grounded in the agent's description, yesterday's summary, and relevant memories. Plans are themselves written back to the memory stream; agents can revise them in response to new observations.

**Why "feed the whole stream" fails:** memory streams grow unboundedly, quickly exceeding context limits; without reflection, the agent has only episodic memory and no abstract self-model, so it can't reason about its own long-term goals; and without planning, it has no temporal coherence and would happily eat lunch three times. The three components jointly solve context-window limits, the absence of abstraction, and temporal consistency.

</details>

**Q76.** **Smallville Paper — Evaluation & Implications (5 pts).** The authors "interviewed" the agents across five areas (self-knowledge, memory, plans, reactions, reflections) and also reported emergent behaviors that arose from a single seed (Isabella's Valentine's Day party). Explain what emerged *without* being scripted, and discuss one concrete UX design implication of this paper for future interactive systems.

<details><summary>Model Answer</summary>

**What emerged unscripted:** From a single seed — Isabella deciding to throw a Valentine's Day party at Hobbs Café — the authors observed that over two simulated days, agents autonomously spread invitations through social conversations, formed new acquaintances, asked each other on dates, and coordinated to arrive at the party at the right time. None of this was scripted. The social chain emerged because each agent retrieved relevant memories (they had heard about the party in conversation), reflected on them, planned around them, and acted accordingly. In the controlled evaluation, ablation studies showed that removing any of observation, reflection, or planning degraded believability — full architecture outperformed ablated variants and outperformed a human baseline on the interview measures.

**UX design implication (choose one):** For interactive systems, generative agents open the door to NPCs, tutoring systems, and prototyping tools that behave with plausible long-term consistency — something scripted agents cannot fake. For UX research specifically, generative agents could serve as *early-stage design probes* (simulated usability panels to pressure-test a flow before recruiting real users). But the paper also cautions that agents can reflect and amplify biases in their underlying LLM, so they must be treated as a signal layered with real user research — not a substitute for it. A responsible design implication is therefore: use generative agents to broaden exploration and stress-test designs, but ground final decisions in research with real humans, since the synthetic users carry the model's biases into any evaluation.

</details>
